\section{Background}
Understanding human behavior requires more than isolated observation of individuals—it demands comprehension of the dynamic interplay between users and their surroundings. Traditional sensing approaches often treat users as isolated entities, analyzing their speech, activities, or physiological signals independently from their environmental context. However, human behavior is fundamentally shaped by environmental factors: our voices propagate through both flesh and air, our activities are embedded in and shaped by surrounding scenarios, and our social interactions are mediated by shared acoustic and spatial contexts. This inherent \textit{user–environment coupling} represents a critical yet underexplored dimension in human-centric sensing research. Properly modeling it enables a broad range of applications, from personalized healthcare and intelligent assistants to context-aware smart devices—shifting the computer's interface from explicit input (keyboard, mouse) to an active understanding of what is happening around the user.

While various sensing methods exist, those based on vision sensors from a third-person perspective—such as pose estimation, activity recognition, and scene understanding—raise significant privacy concerns and are impractical under many deployment conditions. Wearable devices, widely accepted and seamlessly integrated into daily life, offer a compelling alternative. Among them, earphones occupy a uniquely privileged position. Situated at the ear, they sit at the physical intersection of user-generated signals and environmental stimuli: their microphones capture both the wearer's voice at close range and the surrounding soundscape, while their inertial measurement units (IMUs) sense body movement and, crucially, bone-conducted vibrations that propagate exclusively through the wearer's skull. With an estimated 455 million units shipped globally in 2024 (11.2\% year-on-year growth)~\cite{patentlyapple2025audio}, earphones have emerged as a dominant wearable platform and a natural substrate for user–environment coupling research.

This thesis formalizes user–environment coupling across three dimensions, each corresponding to a distinct physical relationship between the user and their surroundings:

\textbf{Acoustic coupling} arises from the fact that a user's vocalization propagates simultaneously through two physically distinct channels: bone conduction (sensed by the IMU accelerometer) and air conduction (sensed by the microphone). These two channels are complementarily coupled—bone conduction carries a user-exclusive, noise-free signal at the cost of bandwidth limitations imposed by hardware, while air conduction delivers broadband audio contaminated by environmental noise. The differentiation task here is to exploit this structured coupling to separate user voice from the ambient acoustic scene. Prior hearing enhancement systems have addressed noise suppression through audio-only solutions~\cite{veluri2023semantic}, but by ignoring the bone-conduction channel they discard a stable, user-specific reference inherent to the device's physical coupling with the body.

\textbf{Contextual coupling} reflects that a user's activities are not independent of their environment: activities generate characteristic near-field sounds, while the environment contributes far-field cues that both reflect and constrain the user's behavior. This bidirectional relationship—user actions shaping and being shaped by environmental context—means that activity and scenario are jointly informative. Prior activity recognition systems leverage motion sensors or audio independently~\cite{gong2023mmg}, missing the structural relationship between near-field user signals and far-field environmental signals. Exploiting contextual coupling requires both differentiating user-generated from environmental sounds and, crucially, re-integrating environmental context as a high-level semantic prior for activity inference.

\textbf{Social coupling} emerges when multiple users share an acoustic environment in conversation. Each participant is simultaneously a sound source and a sensing node, and the set of earphones worn by conversation participants collectively observes a rich, structurally consistent multi-view representation of the same social interaction. Prior work on conversation enhancement treats neighboring speakers as noise to suppress~\cite{chen2024hearable}, discarding the cooperative information latent in their shared acoustic scene. Exploiting social coupling requires both differentiating target from non-target speakers and fostering collaboration among devices to form a coherent, distributed sensing network.

Across all three dimensions, the core sensing challenge has a common structure: \textit{differentiation}—isolating user-specific signals from environmental stimuli—and \textit{collaboration}—leveraging the structured relationship between user and environment (or among multiple users) as a source of complementary information. This thesis addresses both mechanisms and demonstrates how jointly modeling acoustic, contextual, and social coupling on commodity earphones transforms them into comprehensive, privacy-preserving sensing platforms.


\section{Challenges}

Capturing and modeling user–environment coupling involves addressing significant challenges specific to each of the three coupling dimensions introduced above. Within each dimension, the challenges can be characterized along two axes: \textit{differentiation}—isolating user-specific signals from environmental stimuli—and \textit{collaboration}—leveraging the structured relationship between user and environment, or among multiple users, to improve sensing.

\subsection{Acoustic Coupling: Cross-modal Differentiation}
At the acoustic level, the fundamental challenge is to exploit the complementary nature of the bone-conduction and air-conduction channels. Although bone conduction provides a user-exclusive reference immune to environmental noise, the IMU accelerometer on commercial earphones operates at a low sampling rate (typically 1.6~kHz), far below the full bandwidth of human speech (up to 3.4~kHz). Air-conduction microphones cover the full spectrum but cannot distinguish the wearer's voice from competing speakers or ambient noise. Multi-modal fusion of these two heterogeneous signals is non-trivial: the modalities differ in sample rate, spectral coverage, and noise characteristics, and naïve fusion risks the deep network ignoring the low-bandwidth bone-conduction channel in favor of the richer audio signal. Collecting paired multi-modal training data at sufficient scale is also labor-intensive, as no large public dataset of synchronized IMU and microphone recordings from earphones exists. Building a system that is both lightweight enough for on-device inference and robust to the variability of real-world acoustic environments adds further engineering constraints.

\subsection{Contextual Coupling: Spatial Differentiation and Semantic Collaboration}
Contextual coupling poses challenges at two levels. First, \textit{differentiation}: a microphone at the ear receives a superposition of near-field user-generated sounds and far-field environmental sounds that must be spatially separated before either can be reliably interpreted. User motion introduces continuous perturbations to the microphone array geometry, confounding standard localization approaches and creating near-far ambiguities that degrade source separation. Second, \textit{collaboration}: even once separated, integrating short-term activity cues with long-term environmental context requires reasoning across multiple time scales and sensor modalities. The heterogeneity between audio and IMU signals leads to weak cross-modal alignment, and naïve fusion has been shown to underperform unimodal baselines~\cite{huang2022modality}. Furthermore, the long-tail distribution of activities in the wild—spanning dozens of fine-grained categories—makes supervised learning approaches data-hungry and poorly generalizing; a zero-shot capability is highly desirable but technically demanding.

\subsection{Social Coupling: Multi-device Differentiation and Network Collaboration}
Social coupling introduces challenges at both the signal and the system level. For \textit{differentiation}, extracting a target conversation from a mixture of overlapping speakers requires not only robust speaker separation but also a conversation-level understanding of turn-taking and group membership—contextual structure that single-device, speaker-agnostic enhancement methods cannot capture. For \textit{collaboration}, organizing a dynamic set of mobile earphones into a coherent conversation network without any fixed infrastructure is inherently difficult: peer discovery must be performed continuously as participants move, distributed observations from heterogeneous devices must be fused to establish a global coordinate system, and the resulting network topology must remain consistent despite measurement noise and communication unreliability. Beyond network formation, leveraging relay audio from peer devices as conditioning signals for target speech extraction requires bandwidth-efficient protocols and models that are robust to network delay, packet loss, and compression artifacts—all under real-time latency constraints on mobile hardware.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{VibVoice: Bone Conduction-based Speech Enhancement.} We propose and implement a novel speech enhancement system that leverages bone vibrations conducted through the skull, captured by accelerometers widely available in head-mounted wearables. We develop a lightweight multi-modal architecture featuring a two-branch encoder-decoder deep neural network that fuses high-level features from both audio and vibration modalities to reconstruct clean speech. 
    
    To address the challenge of insufficient paired training data, we conduct extensive measurements of bone conduction effects to extract physical impulse responses for cross-modal data augmentation. 
    
    Our evaluation demonstrates that VibVoice achieves up to 21\% improvement in PESQ and 26\% improvement in SNR over state-of-the-art baselines while requiring 72 times less paired data. A user study with 35 participants shows that 87\% prefer VibVoice over the baseline. Additionally, VibVoice achieves 4 to 31 times faster execution on mobile devices compared to baselines.
    
    \item \textbf{EgoLog: Fine-grained Activity Logging with Commercial Earphones.} We propose and implement a sensing system that enables fine-grained activity logging using existing sensors in commercial earphones. The system records both user activities and contextual scenarios, facilitating detailed behavior understanding. EgoLog addresses three key challenges: limited recognition capability due to sparse sensor configurations, ineffective fusion for extracting ambient information, and interference from environmental noise.
    
    Our system architecture comprises a context-aware scenario recognition module and a spatial-aware activity detection module, integrated with other attributes through expert knowledge. Implemented on commercial earphones equipped with binaural microphones and an IMU, EgoLog achieves zero-shot fine-grained activity logging across up to 41 categories, outperforming strong baselines such as ImageBind-LLM by 38\% in F1-score.
    
    \item \textbf{CoHear: Collaborative Conversation-level Speech Enhancement.} We present the first collaborative speech enhancement system that operates at the conversation level across multiple earphones. To enable real-time conversation enhancement, the system performs holistic modeling of all conversation participants and effectively extracts target speech from acoustic mixtures.
    
    CoHear makes two key contributions: (1) a conversation-driven network protocol that enables mobile, infrastructure-free coordination among earphone devices, and (2) a robust target conversation extraction model that leverages relay audio in a bandwidth-efficient manner. 
    
    Evaluation through real-world experiments and simulations demonstrates that our conversation network achieves over 90\% accuracy in group formation, improves speech quality by up to 8.8 dB over state-of-the-art baselines, and maintains real-time performance on mobile devices. A user study with 20 participants confirms CoHear's superior performance and good usability compared to baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
This thesis is structured as follows. The research is organized into two primary domains: audio and activity. Since speech, a form of audio, can be considered a vocal activity, the term "activity" throughout this thesis refers specifically to non-vocal activities.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:related}} provides a comprehensive review of related work and foundational sensing models, covering all the projects included in the thesis. Differently, we reorganize the strucutre from a general perspective, from speech and conversation enhancement, human activity recognition, to acoustic sensing systems.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:background}} introduces essential technical background knowledge that underpins the subsequent chapters. This includes an overview of sensors commonly found in head-mounted wearables, such as microphones, inertial measurement units (IMUs), speakers, and biometric sensors. Additionally, we discuss fundamental algorithms relevant to user-environment sensing, including speech enhancement techniques, activity recognition methods, and acoustic sensing principles. A measurement study is also presented to illustrate key concepts and challenges associated with these technologies.

In the following three chapters, we present our three applications that leverage user-environment coupling using earphones as the sensing platform. For each chapter, we generally have an introduction section, a related work section, and followed by a design section with an evaluation section at the end. For some projects, user study or some background knowledges are also provided. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:vibvoice}} presents our first application, VibVoice, which utilizes motion sensors in earphones to capture bone-conducted vibrations for speech enhancement. By leveraging this unique sensing modality, the system effectively isolates user speech from environmental noise.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:egolog}} introduces our second application, EgoLog, which leverages complementary multi-modal sensing features in earphones to achieve fine-grained activity recognition that is robust to external interference. The system generates detailed activity logs by understanding both user actions and contextual scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:cohear}} presents our third application, CoHear, the first collaborative speech enhancement system for conversation-level processing. The system constructs a conversation network to automatically identify and isolate individual conversations among multiple users wearing earphones.
\end{enumerate}
The chapters are self-contained, and can be read independently, some contents are copied from the published papers.
