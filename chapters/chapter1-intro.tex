\section{Background}

Wearable sensing is often framed as a problem of observing the user: recognizing speech, inferring activity, or estimating physiological state from sensor data. In daily life, however, the user is rarely observed in isolation. Speech travels through both the body and the air. Activities happen in particular places and routines. Conversations unfold among people who share the same acoustic and spatial environment. The signals captured by a wearable device therefore contain both user-generated information and environmental context.

Head-mounted wearables make this relationship especially visible. Devices such as earphones, headphones, AR/VR headsets, and smart glasses are worn close to the mouth, ears, eyes, and head. This placement gives them access to signals that are difficult to capture from wrist-worn or pocket-carried devices: the wearer's speech before it fully disperses into the room, head motion and orientation during interaction, bone-conducted vibration, binaural acoustic cues, and first-person contextual information. At the same time, head-mounted wearables sit directly at the boundary between the user and the environment. Their sensors do not receive a clean user-only stream. They receive mixtures shaped by the user's body, surrounding sound, nearby people, and the social setting.

Throughout this thesis, I use \textit{head-mounted wearables (HMWs)} as the umbrella term for devices worn on the head, including earables, headsets, and smart glasses. \textit{Earables} denotes the ear-worn subset, including earphones and earphone-class prototypes; \textit{mobile devices} denotes companion smartphones and other non-head-mounted platforms. I use HMWs when referring to the shared platform and earables only when the ear-worn form factor matters.

This thesis focuses on earables within the broader class of HMWs, including commodity earphones and earphone-form-factor research prototypes. Earphones are widely used, socially accepted, and already equipped with microphones and inertial measurement units (IMUs). Compared with camera-based devices, they can support continuous sensing while avoiding many of the privacy concerns associated with always-on visual recording. Compared with other wearables, their ear-level placement is unusually well matched to speech, hearing, activity logging, and conversation understanding.

This thesis refers to the above relationship as \textit{user--environment coupling}. The term describes the structured relationship between a user's internal or behavioral state and the surrounding physical, contextual, and social environment as observed by wearable sensors. This coupling is often treated as a source of interference: background noise corrupts speech, ambient sound confuses activity recognition, and overlapping speakers make conversations hard to follow. The central argument of this thesis is different. User--environment coupling should not be treated solely as interference. When modeled explicitly, it can become useful structure for robust, camera-free sensing with lower visual privacy exposure.

The three projects in this thesis are connected by one design question: given a coupled wearable observation, what should the system separate, what should it keep as context, and what should it share across users? To answer this question, the thesis organizes user--environment coupling into three complementary dimensions: \textit{differentiation}, \textit{fusion}, and \textit{collaboration}.

Let $A_u$ denote the user state and $A_e$ denote the environmental state. A head-mounted wearable does not observe either state directly. Instead, its sensors produce an observation $Y$ from user-generated signals, environmental signals, device placement, and sensing noise:
\[
Y = g(\phi_u(A_u), \phi_e(A_e), P, \epsilon),
\]
where $\phi_u(\cdot)$ and $\phi_e(\cdot)$ map latent states to observable signals, $P$ denotes device placement and sensing configuration, and $\epsilon$ denotes noise. This abstraction separates the physical observation process from task-specific inference: the same coupled observation can support different sensing objectives depending on what the system needs to estimate.
For example, in VibVoice, $A_u$ includes the wearer's speech production and $A_e$ includes background speech or noise; in EgoLog, $A_u$ includes the user's activity and $A_e$ includes the surrounding scenario; in CoHear, $A_e$ also includes nearby speakers and their conversational relations.

The first dimension is \textit{differentiation}: estimating a user-specific target $T_u$ from a mixed observation. Directly estimating $T_u = f(Y)$ is generally ill-posed because user and environmental components are entangled. A common strategy is to introduce a user-proximate reference signal $R_u$ and condition the estimator:
\[
\hat{T}_u = f_d(Y, R_u).
\]
In Chapter~\ref{ch:vibvoice}, VibVoice uses bone-conducted vibration as $R_u$ to extract the wearer's speech from noisy microphone audio.

The second dimension is \textit{fusion}: estimating a task-relevant joint representation $Z_{u,e}$ from both user and environmental evidence. In this case, the goal is not simply to remove environmental information. The system should keep the parts of the environment that explain the user's behavior:
\[
\hat{Z}_{u,e} = f_f(Y_{audio}, Y_{motion}, C_e),
\]
where $C_e$ denotes inferred environmental context. A task-specific head then maps this joint representation to an activity or log output:
\[
\hat{T}_{u,e} = q(\hat{Z}_{u,e}).
\]
Here, $C_e$ is an intermediate estimate derived from the coupled observation, rather than an externally provided label. In Chapter~\ref{ch:egolog}, EgoLog uses selective differentiation to separate near-field user sounds from far-field ambient sounds, then fuses activity cues with scenario context for fine-grained daily logging.

The third dimension is \textit{collaboration}: estimating a user target with help from other users' devices and the social structure among them. For user $i$, the system can use the local observation $Y_i$, observations from peer devices $\{Y_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i}$, and a social or conversation graph $G_s$:
\[
\hat{T}_i = f_c(Y_i, \{Y_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i}, G_s).
\]
In Chapter~\ref{ch:cohear}, CoHear estimates $G_s$ from conversation structure and peer-device observations, then uses it to enhance target conversation speech in overlapping-speaker scenes.

Together, these three dimensions form the core design of the thesis. VibVoice shows how to isolate the user from acoustic interference. EgoLog shows how to turn environment from noise into context. CoHear shows how to turn other users from interference into collaborators. Across the three systems, the broader principle is the same: robust wearable sensing depends on modeling the structure of coupling around the user, rather than assuming that the user can be sensed independently from the world.

\section{Challenges}

This conceptual frame leads to a practical question: what aspects of user--environment coupling can be observed with practical HMWs? The answer depends on sensor placement, sensing modality, device constraints, and the target application.

This thesis focuses on earphones because they provide a practical substrate for studying this question. With an estimated 455 million units shipped globally in 2024 (11.2\% year-on-year growth)~\cite{counterpoint2024hearables}, earphones have become one of the most widely deployed wearable platforms. They also sit at the physical intersection of the user and the surrounding environment: their microphones capture both the wearer's voice and the ambient soundscape, while their IMUs capture head motion and body-induced vibration.

Audio and motion sensing are complementary but individually limited. Audio contains rich semantic and acoustic information, but it is easily corrupted by environmental interference and competing speakers. Motion sensing is more user-proximate and less exposed to ambient noise, but it provides indirect evidence of behavior and is sensitive to sensor placement, sampling rate, and inter-user variability. The core technical challenge is to combine these modalities without amplifying the weaknesses of either one.

For \textit{differentiation}, the main challenge is finding an informative reference signal $R_u$ for user-specific separation. Bone-conducted vibration measured by an earphone IMU is useful because it is strongly tied to the wearer and largely immune to ambient noise. However, the accelerometers used in our experiments sample at 1.6~kHz, which is higher than typical consumer earphone IMUs and still below the bandwidth of standard speech audio. Microphone signals preserve full-band acoustic content but cannot reliably isolate the wearer's voice from background noise and competing speakers. VibVoice addresses this gap by using bone-conducted vibration as a clean but bandwidth-limited reference for reconstructing full-band speech from noisy audio.

For \textit{fusion}, the challenge is to decide when environmental information is noise and when it is context. Fine-grained activity logging requires both short-term action cues and longer-term scenario understanding. Yet audio and IMU features are weakly aligned, and na\"ive multimodal fusion can underperform unimodal models~\cite{huang2022modality}. The problem is harder in the wild, where daily activities follow a long-tail distribution and fully supervised training data is expensive to collect. EgoLog addresses this challenge by combining motion-compensated sound localization, contrastive cross-modal learning, and scenario-aware activity inference.

For \textit{collaboration}, the challenge is to model multiple users who share the same acoustic environment. In crowded social scenes, a single device may not have enough information to identify conversation partners or separate the target conversation from overlapping speech. Peer devices must be discovered and coordinated without fixed infrastructure, their observations must be mapped into a shared reference frame, and relayed audio must remain useful under bandwidth, latency, and compression constraints. CoHear addresses these constraints through conversation-driven peer discovery, topology estimation, and bandwidth-aware target conversation enhancement.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A user--environment coupling framework for wearable sensing.} This thesis defines user--environment coupling as the structured relationship between user state and environmental state in wearable sensor observations. It organizes this relationship into three dimensions: \textit{differentiation}, \textit{fusion}, and \textit{collaboration}. This framework provides a single design language for the three systems in the thesis: separate user-specific signals when coupling causes interference, fuse environmental structure when it provides context, and use social coupling when multiple users can cooperate.

    \item \textbf{VibVoice: Bone conduction-based speech enhancement.} We design and implement a multimodal speech enhancement method that combines air-conduction audio with bone-conducted vibration from earphone IMUs. The model is a lightweight two-branch encoder--decoder network that reconstructs clean speech under noise and competing speakers.

    To reduce dependence on paired training data, we measure bone-conduction transfer characteristics and derive impulse responses for cross-modal data augmentation. Experiments show up to 21\% PESQ gain and 26\% SNR gain over representative baselines, using 72$\times$ less paired data. In a user study with 35 participants, 87\% preferred VibVoice to the baseline. On mobile devices, inference is 4$\times$ to 31$\times$ faster.

    \item \textbf{EgoLog: Fine-grained activity logging with HMWs and mobile devices.} We build a fine-grained activity logging method using microphones and IMUs available on common mobile devices and HMWs, with HMWs as the core day-long deployment target. EgoLog models both user activity and environmental scenario, and uses selective differentiation to reduce interference from non-user sounds.

    The method combines motion-compensated sound localization, context-aware scenario recognition, and scenario-aware activity inference. EgoLog supports zero-shot logging across up to 41 categories and improves F1 score by 38\% over strong baselines, including ImageBind-LLM.

    \item \textbf{CoHear: Collaborative conversation-level speech enhancement.} We develop a collaborative speech enhancement method for multi-user conversation settings. CoHear jointly models participants in shared acoustic scenes and extracts target conversation speech from overlapping mixtures.

    CoHear includes a conversation-driven network protocol for infrastructure-free coordination among mobile earphones and an extraction model that uses relay audio under bandwidth constraints. Real-world experiments and simulations show over 90\% group formation accuracy, up to 8.8 dB speech-quality improvement over representative baselines, and real-time mobile performance. A user study with 20 participants further confirms usability and effectiveness.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}

The thesis is organized around the coupling framework rather than around three isolated projects. It spans speech enhancement, activity logging, and conversation enhancement, all using HMWs as the shared sensing platform.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:related}} reviews related work and foundational sensing models relevant to all projects in the thesis. It covers speech and conversation enhancement, human activity recognition, and acoustic sensing systems.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:background}} introduces the technical background needed for the later chapters. It reviews sensors commonly found in HMWs, such as microphones, inertial measurement units (IMUs), speakers, and biometric sensors. It also covers core algorithms used in user--environment sensing, including speech enhancement, activity recognition, and acoustic sensing. Project-specific measurement studies are presented in the corresponding system chapters.

The following three chapters present the main systems built on user--environment coupling:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:vibvoice}} presents VibVoice, which uses bone-conducted vibration from earphone IMUs as a user-specific reference for speech enhancement. This chapter corresponds to the \textit{differentiation} dimension.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:egolog}} presents EgoLog, which combines activity cues and environmental scenario information for fine-grained daily logging. This chapter corresponds to the \textit{fusion} dimension.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:cohear}} presents CoHear, which builds an infrastructure-free multi-earphone conversation network for collaborative speech enhancement. This chapter corresponds to the \textit{collaboration} dimension.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:conclusion}} concludes the thesis and discusses future directions, including unified wearable sensing models, long-term deployment, efficient on-device computation, and privacy-conscious human feedback.
