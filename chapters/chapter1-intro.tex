\section{Background}
Understanding human behavior requires more than isolated observation of individuals—it demands comprehension of the dynamic interplay between users and their surroundings. Traditional sensing approaches often treat users as isolated entities, analyzing their speech, activities, or physiological signals independently from their environmental context. However, human behavior is fundamentally shaped by environmental factors: our conversations are influenced by ambient noise, our activities depend on surrounding scenarios, and our social interactions are mediated by shared acoustic and spatial contexts. This inherent \textit{user-environment coupling} represents a critical yet underexplored dimension in human-centric sensing research.

By understanding human-environment, we can enable enourmous applications, including but not limited to healthcare, personal assistants, smart devices, and more. From the scope of computer science, it can be considered as a way change for the computer to access the information, not only from the keybaords or mouse, but actively understand what's happened around.

While various methods exist to monitor both users and their environments, many rely on vision sensors from a third-person perspective. These approaches, such as pose estimation, human activity recognition, and object or scene recognition, often raise significant privacy concerns and are impractical in many scenarios. In contrast, wearable devices, which are widely accepted and seamlessly integrated into daily life, offer a promising alternative.

Earphones, particularly true wireless stereo (TWS) earphones exemplified by the Apple AirPods series, present a unique opportunity to address this coupling. With an estimated global shipment of 455 million units in 2024, reflecting an 11.2\% year-on-year growth~\cite{patentlyapple2025audio}, earphones have emerged as the next major wearable platform following smartphones. Unlike other wearables, earphones occupy a distinctive position at the intersection of user-generated signals and environmental stimuli, making them naturally suited for capturing user-environment coupling. Their omnidirectional microphones simultaneously capture both user-generated sounds (speech, vocal activities) and environmental sounds (ambient noise, other speakers' voices), providing a holistic acoustic view of both entities. Meanwhile, their proximity to sensory organs enables privileged access to user-specific signals: microphones receive voice with superior signal-to-noise ratios, while motion sensors capture bone-conducted vibrations that propagate exclusively through the wearer's body. This unique combination allows earphones to effectively distinguish and model the dynamic interplay between user and environment.

Despite these advantages, prior research has predominantly concentrated on the independent utilization of individual sensors (e.g., microphones or inertial measurement units) in earphones, often overlooking their potential for holistic user-environment sensing. For instance, hearing enhancement systems have explored methods to suppress noise or amplify desired sounds, but these approaches typically focus on audio-only solutions, limiting their adaptability to dynamic environments~\cite{veluri2023semantic}. Similarly, acoustic augmented reality applications have demonstrated the ability to spatially align audio streams with user orientation, yet they often rely on predefined triggers and static contexts, which are insufficient for capturing the fluidity of real-world interactions~\cite{yang2020ear}. Activity recognition systems, on the other hand, have leveraged motion sensors to detect user actions, but these efforts frequently lack the integration of environmental cues, resulting in coarse-grained recognition~\cite{gong2023mmg}.

In this thesis, we investigate the potential of leveraging existing sensors on head-mounted wearables to capture and model user-environment coupling through two fundamental dimensions: (1) distinguishing between user-specific signals and environmental stimuli, and (2) fostering a collaborative interaction between the user and their environment. This perspective emphasizes that user-environment coupling is inherently bidirectional, reflecting a dynamic and holistic relationship rather than a one-way interaction.
We demonstrate how head-mounted wearables (e.g., earphones) can serve as a powerful platform for capturing this coupling across three applications: (1) speech enhancement, (2) activity logging, and (3) conversation enhancement. Each application exemplifies a distinct aspect of user-environment coupling:
where we emphasize the differentiation of user and environment in the speech enhancement, all the sounds except the user's voice are considered as noises that are expected to be suppressed. In the activity logging, we focus on understanding both user activities and contextual scenarios, highlighting the interplay between user actions and environmental contexts. Finally, in conversation enhancement, we explore the collaborative dynamics among multiple users within shared acoustic environments, emphasizing the social dimension of user-environment coupling.


\section{Challenges}

Capturing and modeling user-environment coupling involves addressing significant challenges across two fundamental dimensions: differentiation and collaboration as follows. These dimensions reflect the dual nature of user-environment interactions, where the ability to distinguish between user-specific signals and environmental stimuli is as critical as fostering meaningful interactions between the two. 

\subsection{Differentiation}
Differentiation involves isolating user-specific signals from the surrounding environmental stimuli. This task is inherently challenging due to the dynamic and often unpredictable nature of real-world environments.
For \textbf{speech enhancement}, the primary challenge lies in distinguishing the user's voice from ambient noise and competing speakers. Wearable devices, such as earphones, must achieve this in real-time despite hardware limitations, including low-quality microphones and constrained processing power. The variability of acoustic environments, such as fluctuating noise levels and overlapping speech, further complicates this task.
In \textbf{activity logging}, differentiation requires identifying user actions while simultaneously recognizing the contextual scenarios in which they occur. This involves integrating data from multiple sensors, such as motion and audio, to infer meaningful patterns. However, the sparsity and variability of sensor data, coupled with interference from environmental noise, make it difficult to achieve robust and accurate recognition of both user activities and environmental contexts.
For \textbf{conversation enhancement}, the challenge is to isolate individual users' voices in shared acoustic environments. This requires wearable devices to dynamically identify and separate multiple speakers, even in the presence of overlapping speech and background noise. Achieving this differentiation in real-time is critical for enhancing the quality of social interactions.

\subsection{Collaboration}
Collaboration focuses on fostering dynamic interactions between users and their environments, as well as among multiple users. This dimension emphasizes the bidirectional and holistic nature of user-environment coupling.
For \textbf{activity logging}, collaboration entails understanding the interplay between user actions and environmental contexts. This involves capturing long-term, continuous scenarios rather than isolated events. Wearable devices must integrate diverse sensor modalities to infer meaningful patterns, while also accounting for the fluid and dynamic nature of real-world interactions.
In \textbf{conversation enhancement}, collaboration extends to the social dimension, where multiple users interact within shared acoustic environments. Wearable devices must dynamically coordinate and communicate to identify users engaged in the same social context, such as a conversation. This requires efficient algorithms for grouping users, enhancing social signals, and maintaining real-time performance in dynamic, multi-user scenarios.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{VibVoice: Bone Conduction-based Speech Enhancement.} We propose and implement a novel speech enhancement system that leverages bone vibrations conducted through the skull, captured by accelerometers widely available in head-mounted wearables. We develop a lightweight multi-modal architecture featuring a two-branch encoder-decoder deep neural network that fuses high-level features from both audio and vibration modalities to reconstruct clean speech. 
    
    To address the challenge of insufficient paired training data, we conduct extensive measurements of bone conduction effects to extract physical impulse responses for cross-modal data augmentation. 
    
    Our evaluation demonstrates that VibVoice achieves up to 21\% improvement in PESQ and 26\% improvement in SNR over state-of-the-art baselines while requiring 72 times less paired data. A user study with 35 participants shows that 87\% prefer VibVoice over the baseline. Additionally, VibVoice achieves 4 to 31 times faster execution on mobile devices compared to baselines.
    
    \item \textbf{EgoLog: Fine-grained Activity Logging with Commercial Earphones.} We propose and implement a sensing system that enables fine-grained activity logging using existing sensors in commercial earphones. The system records both user activities and contextual scenarios, facilitating detailed behavior understanding. EgoLog addresses three key challenges: limited recognition capability due to sparse sensor configurations, ineffective fusion for extracting ambient information, and interference from environmental noise.
    
    Our system architecture comprises a context-aware scenario recognition module and a spatial-aware activity detection module, integrated with other attributes through expert knowledge. Implemented on commercial earphones equipped with binaural microphones and an IMU, EgoLog achieves zero-shot fine-grained activity logging across up to 41 categories, outperforming strong baselines such as ImageBind-LLM by 38\% in F1-score.
    
    \item \textbf{CoHear: Collaborative Conversation-level Speech Enhancement.} We present the first collaborative speech enhancement system that operates at the conversation level across multiple earphones. To enable real-time conversation enhancement, the system performs holistic modeling of all conversation participants and effectively extracts target speech from acoustic mixtures.
    
    CoHear makes two key contributions: (1) a conversation-driven network protocol that enables mobile, infrastructure-free coordination among earphone devices, and (2) a robust target conversation extraction model that leverages relay audio in a bandwidth-efficient manner. 
    
    Evaluation through real-world experiments and simulations demonstrates that our conversation network achieves over 90\% accuracy in group formation, improves speech quality by up to 8.8 dB over state-of-the-art baselines, and maintains real-time performance on mobile devices. A user study with 20 participants confirms CoHear's superior performance and good usability compared to baselines.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
This thesis is structured as follows. The research is organized into two primary domains: audio and activity. Since speech, a form of audio, can be considered a vocal activity, the term "activity" throughout this thesis refers specifically to non-vocal activities.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:related}} provides a comprehensive review of related work and foundational sensing models, covering all the projects included in the thesis. Differently, we reorganize the strucutre from a general perspective, from speech and conversation enhancement, human activity recognition, to acoustic sensing systems.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:background}} introduces essential technical background knowledge that underpins the subsequent chapters. This includes an overview of sensors commonly found in head-mounted wearables, such as microphones, inertial measurement units (IMUs), speakers, and biometric sensors. Additionally, we discuss fundamental algorithms relevant to user-environment sensing, including speech enhancement techniques, activity recognition methods, and acoustic sensing principles. A measurement study is also presented to illustrate key concepts and challenges associated with these technologies.

In the following three chapters, we present our three applications that leverage user-environment coupling using earphones as the sensing platform. For each chapter, we generally have an introduction section, a related work section, and followed by a design section with an evaluation section at the end. For some projects, user study or some background knowledges are also provided. 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:vibvoice}} presents our first application, VibVoice, which utilizes motion sensors in earphones to capture bone-conducted vibrations for speech enhancement. By leveraging this unique sensing modality, the system effectively isolates user speech from environmental noise.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:egolog}} introduces our second application, EgoLog, which leverages complementary multi-modal sensing features in earphones to achieve fine-grained activity recognition that is robust to external interference. The system generates detailed activity logs by understanding both user actions and contextual scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:cohear}} presents our third application, CoHear, the first collaborative speech enhancement system for conversation-level processing. The system constructs a conversation network to automatically identify and isolate individual conversations among multiple users wearing earphones.
\end{enumerate}
The chapters are self-contained, and can be read independently, some contents are copied from the published papers.
