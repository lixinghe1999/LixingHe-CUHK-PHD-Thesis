\section{Background}
Understanding human behavior requires more than observing a person in isolation; it also requires modeling how the person and the surrounding environment affect each other. Traditional sensing methods often analyze speech, activity, or physiological signals without explicitly modeling environmental context. In practice, however, behavior depends heavily on context: speech propagates through both tissue and air, activities happen in specific settings, and social interaction unfolds in shared acoustic and spatial environments. In this thesis, I refer to this relationship as \textit{user--environment coupling}. Although it is central to human-centric sensing, it remains underexplored. Modeling this coupling can support applications such as personalized healthcare, intelligent assistants, and context-aware devices that move beyond explicit user input toward a better understanding of everyday situations.

To formalize this perspective, this thesis introduces three coupling dimensions that correspond to our three systems: \textit{differentiation}, \textit{fusion}, and \textit{collaboration}. Each dimension reflects a distinct physical relationship between users and their environments and introduces a different sensing challenge.

Let $A_{user}$ denote the user state and $A_{env}$ the environmental state. Although $A_{user}$ is not directly observable, it produces user-specific signals $S_{user}$ embedded in the sensor observation $S_{mix}$. The environment contributes $S_{env}$, yielding:
\[
S_{mix} = g(S_{user}, S_{env}),
\]
where $g$ denotes the physical mixing process captured by sensors.

The first problem is \textit{differentiation}: recovering $S_{user}$ from the mixture. Direct recovery via $S_{user} = f(S_{mix})$ is generally ill-posed because user and environmental components are entangled. A common strategy is to introduce a reference signal $S_{ref}$ derived from a user-proximate sensor and condition the separation model:
\[
S_{user} = f(S_{mix}, S_{ref}).
\]
$S_{ref}$ may be weakly informative (e.g., a noise estimate) or strongly correlated with user speech. In Chapter~\ref{ch:vibvoice}, we use bone-conduction signals as a user-specific $S_{ref}$ to extract target speech from noisy audio.

User--environment interaction is not only something to suppress; it can also provide useful structure. We denote this shared structure by $A_{fusion}$, a function of both $A_{user}$ and $A_{env}$. In general, $A_{fusion}$ is not equivalent to $S_{mix}$, but rather to an informative subset of it. Estimating $A_{fusion}$ supports signal separation and downstream inference tasks where context matters. In Chapter~\ref{ch:egolog}, we model contextual coupling between activity and scene to derive $A_{fusion}$ and improve fine-grained activity logging.

The final dimension is \textit{collaboration}, where multiple users coexist in the same environment. In this setting, each user is coupled with both the environment and other users. We represent this social structure as $A_{social}$ and incorporate it into the separation objective:
\[
S_{user} = f(S_{mix}, S_{ref}, A_{fusion}, A_{social}).
\]
In Chapter~\ref{ch:cohear}, we estimate $A_{social}$ from conversation structure among participants and use it to extract target speech from overlapping speakers.

In summary, the three dimensions share one principle: robust sensing depends on isolating user-specific signals while also using structured coupling as complementary information. This thesis shows that jointly modeling acoustic, contextual, and social coupling can turn commodity earphones into practical and privacy-preserving sensing platforms.

\section{Challenges}

This formulation leaves a practical question: how should we define $g$? From an information engineering perspective, the answer is constrained by the sensors available to us. The sensors, their capabilities, and the corresponding system design ultimately determine which applications are feasible.
In this thesis, we focus on the sensing capabilities of head-mounted wearables, with particular emphasis on earphones. This choice is motivated by the ubiquity of earphones and their unique sensing capabilities, which enable rich user-environment coupling. With an estimated 455 million units shipped globally in 2024 (11.2\% year-on-year growth)~\cite{counterpoint2024hearables}, earphones have emerged as a dominant wearable platform and a natural substrate for user–environment coupling research.
Earphones sit at the physical intersection of user-generated signals and environmental stimuli: their microphones capture both the wearer's voice and the surrounding soundscape, while their inertial measurement units (IMUs) sense body movement.
Smart glasses add visual sensing, which is powerful but often raises stronger privacy concerns and consumes more power. To keep the thesis focused on widely available commercial devices, we therefore concentrate on two sensing modalities that already exist on many wearables: audio and motion.

Audio and motion sensing are complementary but individually limited. Audio is easily corrupted by environmental interference, whereas motion signals provide only indirect evidence of user behavior and are sensitive to noise and inter-user variability. The core challenge is to use this cross-modal complementarity without amplifying the weaknesses of either modality. That requires robust fusion algorithms, principled signal processing, and scalable data-collection pipelines that generalize to real-world settings.

From the perspective of \textit{differentiation}, the central problem is identifying an informative reference signal $S_{ref}$ for user-specific separation. The bone-conduction channel measured by the IMU accelerometer offers a user-exclusive cue that is largely immune to ambient noise. However, the accelerometers used in our experiments sample at 1.6~kHz, which is higher than typical consumer earphone IMUs (often 100--200~Hz). At those lower rates, the IMU data cannot capture the full speech bandwidth (up to 3.4~kHz). By contrast, air-conduction microphones preserve full-band acoustic content but cannot reliably isolate the wearer's speech from competing speakers and background noise.

For \textit{fusion}, the challenge is to model structured coupling between user and environment to improve downstream inference. In activity recognition, this requires integrating short-term action cues with long-term contextual information across modalities and timescales. Yet weak alignment between audio and IMU representations often degrades cross-modal learning, and naïve fusion can underperform unimodal approaches~\cite{huang2022modality}. This challenge is compounded by the long-tail distribution of fine-grained activities in the wild, which makes fully supervised solutions data-intensive.

Finally, \textit{collaboration} requires structured modeling of interactions among multiple users. In social acoustic scenes, extracting a target conversation from overlapping speech requires source separation as well as conversation-level modeling of turn-taking and group membership, which single-device, speaker-agnostic methods cannot provide. Peer devices must be discovered and coordinated without dedicated infrastructure, their heterogeneous observations must be fused into a shared reference frame, and relayed audio must remain usable despite bandwidth, latency, and compression constraints.

\section{Contributions}

The main contributions of this thesis are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{A formal definition of user--environment coupling for wearable sensing.} We define user--environment coupling as the structured relationship between user state and environmental state in sensor observations, and organize it into three dimensions: \textit{differentiation}, \textit{fusion}, and \textit{collaboration}. This definition gives a single framework for separating user-specific signals from mixed observations while still using contextual and social structure when it is informative.

    \item \textbf{VibVoice: Bone conduction-based speech enhancement.} We design and implement a multimodal speech enhancement method that combines air-conduction audio with bone-conducted vibration from earphone IMUs. The model is a lightweight two-branch encoder-decoder network that reconstructs clean speech under noise and competing speakers.

    To reduce dependence on paired training data, we measure bone-conduction transfer characteristics and derive impulse responses for cross-modal data augmentation. Experiments show up to 21\% PESQ gain and 26\% SNR gain over representative baselines, using 72$\times$ less paired data. In a user study with 35 participants, 87\% preferred VibVoice to the baseline. On mobile devices, inference is 4$\times$ to 31$\times$ faster.

    \item \textbf{EgoLog: Fine-grained activity logging with commercial earphones.} We build a fine-grained activity logging method using microphones and IMUs already available in commercial earphones. EgoLog records user activities together with contextual scenarios, and tackles sparse sensing, weak cross-modal alignment, and environmental interference in everyday settings.

    The method combines context-aware scenario recognition, spatial-aware activity detection, and attribute-level integration based on expert knowledge. EgoLog supports zero-shot logging across up to 41 categories and improves F1 score by 38\% over strong baselines, including ImageBind-LLM.

    \item \textbf{CoHear: Collaborative conversation-level speech enhancement.} We develop a collaborative speech enhancement method for multi-user conversation settings. CoHear jointly models participants in shared acoustic scenes and extracts target conversation speech from overlapping mixtures.

    CoHear includes a conversation-driven network protocol for infrastructure-free coordination (i.e., without requiring a base station or centralized server) among mobile earphones and an extraction model that uses relay audio under bandwidth constraints. Real-world experiments and simulations show over 90\% group formation accuracy, up to 8.8 dB speech-quality improvement over representative baselines, and real-time mobile performance. A user study with 20 participants further confirms usability and effectiveness.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Thesis Outline}
This thesis is structured as follows. The research is organized into two primary domains: audio and activity.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:related}} reviews related work and foundational sensing models relevant to all projects in the thesis. Rather than following the structure of any single paper, it is organized from a broader perspective, covering speech and conversation enhancement, human activity recognition, and acoustic sensing systems.

\textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:background}} introduces the technical background needed for the later chapters. It reviews sensors commonly found in head-mounted wearables, such as microphones, inertial measurement units (IMUs), speakers, and biometric sensors. It also covers the core algorithms used in user--environment sensing, including speech enhancement, activity recognition, and acoustic sensing. A measurement study is included to illustrate the key concepts and practical challenges.

The following three chapters present the main applications built on user--environment coupling with earphones as the sensing platform. Each chapter generally includes an introduction, a related work section, a system design, and an evaluation. Some chapters also include project-specific background or user studies.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:vibvoice}} presents our first application, VibVoice, which utilizes motion sensors in earphones to capture bone-conducted vibrations for speech enhancement. By leveraging this unique sensing modality, the system effectively isolates user speech from environmental noise.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:egolog}} introduces our second application, EgoLog, which leverages complementary multi-modal sensing features in earphones to achieve fine-grained activity recognition that is robust to external interference. The system generates detailed activity logs by understanding both user actions and contextual scenarios.
    \item \textbf{Chapter \ref{ch:cohear}} presents our third application, CoHear, the first collaborative speech enhancement system for conversation-level processing. The system constructs a conversation network to automatically identify and isolate individual conversations among multiple users wearing earphones.
\end{enumerate}

The chapters are largely self-contained and can be read independently. Some material is adapted from the corresponding published papers.
