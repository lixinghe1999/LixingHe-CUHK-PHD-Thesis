This chapter covers the technical foundations of user--environment sensing on head-mounted wearables. We first survey the sensors available on these devices, focusing on the properties that matter for our system designs. We then introduce the core algorithmic framework using the notation established in Chapter~1. Measurement studies that validate specific design choices are deferred to each project chapter (Chapters~\ref{ch:vibvoice}--\ref{ch:cohear}), where they appear alongside the relevant system context.

\noindent\textbf{Microphone.}
Microphones on head-mounted wearables are typically small, omnidirectional MEMS elements. Their compact form factor limits sensitivity and frequency response compared to professional-grade equipment, but their placement near the ear canal captures both the wearer's voice (via tissue and air conduction) and the ambient soundscape. Throughout this thesis, the microphone provides the primary acoustic observation $S_{mix}$.

\noindent\textbf{IMU.}
Inertial measurement units (IMUs) on wearables include accelerometers and gyroscopes. For this thesis, the accelerometer is the more critical sensor: it captures bone-conducted vibrations produced by the wearer's own speech, providing a user-exclusive signal that is largely immune to ambient noise. However, the sampling rate of commodity IMUs (100--200~Hz in consumer earphones) limits speech bandwidth. In our experiments, we use a higher-rate IMU (1.6~kHz) to explore the potential of this modality. The accelerometer also captures body motion for activity recognition (Chapter~\ref{ch:egolog}).

\noindent\textbf{Camera and others.}
Cameras offer rich visual context but raise privacy and power concerns; we therefore design systems that do not assume cameras. Speakers can be repurposed for active acoustic sensing (e.g., ultrasonic echolocation), but this requires hardware access not available on all devices. Biometric sensors (PPG, temperature) are outside this thesis's scope.

\section{Core Algorithms}

We now formalize the algorithmic framework using the notation from Chapter~1. Let $S_{user}$ denote the user-specific signal of interest (e.g., speech or motion), $S_{env}$ the environmental contribution, and $S_{mix}$ the mixed sensor observation:
\[
S_{mix} = g(S_{user}, S_{env}),
\]
where $g$ captures the physical mixing process. Recovery of $S_{user}$ is the central technical challenge, addressed through the three coupling dimensions.

\subsection{Sound Enhancement and Separation}

In the additive noise model common to acoustic sensing, $S_{mix} = S_{user} + S_{env}$, where $S_{user}$ is clean speech and $S_{env}$ is background noise. A separation model $f$ estimates the target:
\[
\hat{S}_{user} = f(S_{mix}).
\]
This formulation is ill-posed without additional structure. A common strategy is to introduce an auxiliary reference signal $S_{ref}$ (e.g., bone-conduction vibration from the IMU) that correlates with $S_{user}$ but not $S_{env}$:
\[
\hat{S}_{user} = f(S_{mix}, S_{ref}).
\]
This corresponds to the \textit{differentiation} dimension of Chapter~1. In Chapter~\ref{ch:vibvoice}, the IMU accelerometer provides $S_{ref}$ as bone-conducted vibration, and a two-branch encoder--decoder network computes $f$.

Traditional methods (spectral subtraction, Wiener filtering, beamforming) rely on parametric noise models or spatial statistics. Deep learning approaches learn $f$ directly from data, often achieving better generalization at the cost of domain sensitivity. The target extraction paradigm further conditions on $S_{ref}$ to handle dynamic, multi-speaker scenarios.

\subsection{Multi-channel Audio Processing}

When multiple microphones are available, spatial cues such as time difference of arrival (TDoA) and direction of arrival (DoA) provide additional structure. The TDoA between microphone pair $(i,j)$ for a source at position $\mathbf{s}$ is:
\[
\tau_{ij} = \frac{\|\mathbf{s} - \mathbf{p}_i\| - \|\mathbf{s} - \mathbf{p}_j\|}{c},
\]
where $\mathbf{p}_i,\mathbf{p}_j$ are microphone positions and $c$ is the speed of sound. The GCC function~\cite{knapp1976generalized} estimates $\tau_{ij}$ from cross-correlation. For binaural earphone microphones, the head-related transfer function (HRTF) introduces frequency-dependent spatial cues that can resolve front--back ambiguity.

In multi-user settings, individual devices form a wireless acoustic sensor network (WASN). Each node observes sources in its local coordinate frame, and global geometry calibration fuses these observations. This supports the \textit{collaboration} dimension of Chapter~1, enabling devices to share relay audio and spatial context. Detailed formulations of SSL and WASN are provided in Chapter~\ref{ch:cohear} and the CoHear system description.

\subsection{Human Activity Recognition}

For non-vocal activities, the IMU provides the primary signal. HAR on wearable devices treats the $6 \times T$ accelerometer--gyroscope time series as input to a classifier. The challenge is that IMU-only sensing lacks environmental and semantic context, which is where the \textit{fusion} dimension applies: combining IMU motion signals with ambient audio (Chapter~\ref{ch:egolog}) captures both what the user is doing and in what scenario. Detailed HAR algorithms and their evaluation appear in Chapter~\ref{ch:egolog}.

\section{Summary}

The sensors and algorithms introduced here form the technical substrate for Chapters~\ref{ch:vibvoice}--\ref{ch:cohear}. The microphone--IMU pair drives all three systems: bone-conduction sensing enables \textit{differentiation} of user speech, cross-modal fusion enables \textit{activity recognition}, and WASN-style geometry enables \textit{collaborative enhancement}. Each subsequent chapter builds on this foundation with project-specific measurement studies, system designs, and evaluations.
